# Methodology (main text)

We describe five components of our agentic transition-state discovery
pipeline: a benchmark of elementary reaction steps; the agentic
sampling stage that generates the initial guesses; an unsupervised
atom-mapping stage that brings reactant, product, ground-truth TS,
and every initial-guess (IG) TS into a shared atom indexing; a
verifier that ranks IG-TS structures by combining mode-derived
features with a diversity penalty; and the evaluation metrics.
Algorithmic details, hyperparameters, and ablations are in
Appendix A; full equations and tolerances are in Appendix B.

## Benchmark

We evaluate on a curated benchmark of 155 elementary reaction
steps. Each step is a single elementary reaction R → P with a
literature- or DFT-derived reference transition state (the "ground
truth", GT) and 20 initial-guess TS structures generated by an
upstream LLM-driven agent layer (described in [§ XX]). Step sizes
range from 18 to 149 atoms (median 57); chemistry domains span
organocatalysis, transition-metal catalysis, pericyclic, and
main-group reactions; element coverage includes the first three
periodic-table rows plus selected 4d/5d transition metals. Per-step
broken/formed bond counts are usually small (most steps have one
broken and one formed bond). For every geometry we compute a
GFN2-xTB single-point Wiberg bond order matrix and a Hessian; both
are cached so downstream evaluation is essentially zero-cost after
a one-time benchmark sweep. Full benchmark statistics, deduplication
notes, and chemistry breakdowns are in Appendix A.1.

## Sampling

Initial-guess transition states are produced by an agentic sampling
stage. For every elementary step we spawn $n = 20$ independent
LLM-driven agents, each given the reactant and product xyz files
and asked to construct a transition-state 3D geometry. Each agent
operates a small toolkit of geometry-editing actions and saves its
proposal as `ts_guess_final.xyz`; the 20 outputs constitute the
initial-guess pool that the rest of the pipeline ranks. Spawns are
fully independent so the pool naturally covers diverse chemical
hypotheses and conformers without any coordination between
agents. The system prompt, the action set the agents can call, and
the LLM identity used in this work are in Appendix A.3.

## Atom mapping

Reactant and product xyz files coming out of xtb do not carry an
atom correspondence — atom *i* in R is not necessarily atom *i* in P.
Every downstream comparison (bond classification, mode alignment,
oracle alignment) requires this correspondence, so we infer it from
chemistry. We use the Wiberg bond order matrix as the chemical
fingerprint and grow a mapping atom-by-atom through a priority-queue
propagation over the WBO graph. Strong bonds (highest WBO) are
matched first; weaker bonds confirm or refute the partial mapping;
when the available evidence cannot distinguish between candidate
matches (set-non-unique saturation) the algorithm branches and
explores hypotheses in parallel. Surviving branches are scored
lexicographically by (a) the number of broken plus formed bonds —
preferring the simplest reactive change consistent with the data —
(b) the count of spectator stereocentre chirality violations to
break ties between geometrically valid but chirality-inverted
mappings, and (c) the number of mapped atoms. Bond classification
follows immediately from the WBO change between R and P at each
mapped pair. The same mapping is computed for every TS structure
(GT and 20 IGs) so that index *i* refers to the same chemical atom
across the 23 structures of a given step. Algorithm details
(PQ-pop semantics, set-uniqueness, branching rules, tolerances) are
in Appendix B.1.

## Verifier

The verifier ranks the 20 IG-TS structures and surfaces a top-*k*
set; in production *k* = 2 because the chemist reviews two
candidates per step. Three sign-blind, $[0, 1]$-valued mode
features are computed for every normal mode of every IG: a
**bond-direction overlap** that projects the mode onto the unit
broken/formed-bond vectors at the IG's TS coordinates (with sign
chosen so that broken-bond endpoints separating and formed-bond
endpoints approaching gives a positive contribution), a
**reaction-coordinate overlap** that projects the mode onto the
core-atom-restricted R→P displacement, and a **core mass fraction**
that measures how much of the mode's squared amplitude lives on
atoms that touch a reactive bond. Each IG is collapsed to its
imaginary mode of highest bond-overlap and filtered by two
structural priors (one or two imaginary modes; rxn-overlap above a
small threshold) so that IGs without a reaction-direction-aligned
saddle character are dropped. The score combines all three features
multiplicatively with a soft preference for clean first-order
saddles.

The top-*k* selection is greedy with a multiplicative diversity
penalty: each subsequent pick is discounted by its mass-weighted
cosine similarity to all already-selected picks. The penalty drives
the second pick to a different basin from the first — when two IGs
converged to the same TS structure the multiplicative discount
collapses the candidate's score to near zero, so the second slot is
always exploited for an alternative chemical hypothesis rather than
duplicating the top one. This diversity term is the dominant
contributor to the verifier's pass@2 improvement over a plain
bond-overlap ranking. Full feature definitions, the score formula,
and hyperparameter values are in Appendix B.2.

## Evaluation

For each step we measure quality by the **oracle alignment score** —
the sign-blind cosine similarity in 3*N*-Cartesian displacement
space between an IG mode and the GT mode, after both have been
re-indexed into the shared R-frame. The metric is high (≥ 0.95)
when the IG's mode and the GT mode describe the same nuclear
motion, and decays smoothly as the two diverge. We aggregate this
in two ways. First, **pass@*k*** is the cumulative max of oracle
alignment over the verifier's first *k* picks; this is what the
chemist effectively sees. Second, we report a **uniform-random
baseline**: the closed-form expected pass@*k* of a verifier with no
signal, sampled without replacement from the 20-IG pool. The lift
of pass@*k* over the random baseline isolates the signal contributed
by the verifier independently of how good the agent layer's IGs
already are. Pass@*k* numbers are paired with **human evaluation**:
for every step the chemist marks each surfaced IG correct or
incorrect; a step passes under human judgement if at least one of
the two surfaced IGs is correct. Definitions, exact formulas, and
the closed-form expectation are in Appendix B.3.
